Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Lucia Si

w5si@ucsd.edu

Data year: 2020 · Source: Guttmacher Institute · Visualizations created in Tableau

Proposition

Geographic access to abortion clinics in the United States was substantially limited in 2020.

Visualization 1:FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale

Scores range from -2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  • Frame coverage as absence. Score: -0.5. I emphasize women and counties without a clinic, which directs attention toward gaps in geographic coverage. The labels describe the measures accurately, but presenting only the absence side encourages a more negative interpretation. Showing coverage instead would highlight the opposite story, as demonstrated in the opposing visualization.
  • Rank states by the uncovered population share. Score: +1. Sorting the bars from highest to lowest makes differences easier to compare and places the largest gaps first. An alphabetical order would make individual states easier to find, but would communicate the overall ranking less clearly. The long chart preserves the state comparisons, although it requires scrolling.
  • Pair population and county measures. Score: -1. The bar chart shows the share of women living in counties without a clinic, while the map shows the share of counties without one. Together, they make the problem appear both personal and geographically widespread. Using only the population measure would give a narrower argument; however, pairing them risks making two different denominators seem like interchangeable evidence.
  • Use a choropleth to emphasize geographic extent. Score: -1. The map makes regional patterns easy to recognize, but large states occupy more visual space regardless of how many people live there. The county percentage also gives each county equal weight despite population differences. An equal-sized state grid would reduce the area effect, while the geographic map makes the spatial argument more immediate.

Visualization 2:AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale

Scores range from -2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  • Present coverage rather than absence. Score: -0.5. The bar chart shows the percentage of women living in counties with a clinic, the complement of the percentage without one. This emphasizes existing coverage and creates a more reassuring impression without changing the underlying population information. Showing both percentages together would make the framing more transparent, but would weaken the focus of this argument.
  • Place the highest coverage values first. Score: +1. Descending order makes comparisons straightforward and introduces readers to the states with the strongest coverage first. Alphabetical order would help readers locate a particular state, but would make the overall pattern harder to follow. The ranking communicates differences clearly, although the long chart means lower-coverage states receive less immediate attention.
  • Use within-state care as evidence of access. Score: -1.5. The map highlights the share of abortion recipients who received care in their home state. This supports the impression that care was available without crossing state borders, but staying within a state does not necessarily mean a short or easy trip. Travel time would measure geographic burden more directly; the selected measure instead makes state boundaries seem like a meaningful threshold for access.
  • Focus on people who successfully obtained care. Score: -1.5. The map's denominator includes residents who obtained abortions, leaving out people who needed care but could not obtain it. This makes successful access more visible than unmet need and strengthens the opposing argument. A measure including everyone who sought care would address that limitation, but this chart does not provide that information. Its values describe where recipients received care, not whether everyone could access it.

Final Reflection

The straightforward part of this project was using the same dataset to show both clinic presence and clinic absence. The harder part was making the two arguments persuasive without simply repeating the same chart with a different title. I tried a circular layout, but returned to horizontal bars because comparisons across states were easier to read. Pairing those bars with maps gave each argument a geographic perspective, although the long bar charts require scrolling.

What surprised me most was how much the definition of access changes the story. A high percentage of counties without clinics can suggest widespread exclusion, while a population-based measure can show that clinics are located where many women live. These findings can coexist because counties have different populations. The opposing map makes another shift: receiving care within one's home state sounds reassuring, but it does not establish that the journey was short or affordable. It also leaves out people who needed an abortion but never obtained one. The numbers can be accurate while the conclusion they encourage goes beyond what they measure.

I now understand ethical visualization as making an argument while keeping its evidence and limitations understandable. Sorting values, choosing a clear chart, and emphasizing a relevant finding can be acceptable persuasive choices. The boundary is crossed when the presentation encourages readers to confuse county coverage with individual access, or successful care with access for everyone. Accurate labels help, but they do not automatically correct a misleading overall impression. For these visualizations, that means keeping the denominators and the 2020 date visible, acknowledging what each measure leaves out, and avoiding claims that these charts describe every aspect of access or present-day conditions.
